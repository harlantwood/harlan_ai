# How to use these

**Do not put thirteen testimonials on the page.** A wall of praise reads as insecurity and nobody
reads past the third. Choose three or four that each do a *different* job, and consider a
tasteful "more" affordance only if it doesn't turn into a scroll trap.

A strong minimal set, one per axis:

| Axis | Quote |
| --- | --- |
| Mastery and character | **Jim McCarthy** — "the discipline and impact of a seasoned master" |
| Technical + human synthesis, and an explicit consulting endorsement | **Adam Apollo** — "my first choice for consultation" |
| Business outcomes and executive translation | **Greg Woodward** — "getting both groups into alignment" |
| Reliability under pressure | **Patrick Donohoe** — "when I bring him an urgent problem, it will be solved, every time" |

Hold **Noah Thorp** ("impeccable personal integrity") in reserve — it may serve the values-aligned
audience better than Woodward's business framing. **Kevin Suffern** is the best option if you want
one that's surprising and human rather than commercial.

**Placement.** Resist the standard three-across testimonial card grid — it's the most template-y
component in web design and it will fight the design language. Better: let one or two quotes
breathe as full-width pull quotes at the moments where a reader would naturally feel doubt (right
after the capability claims; right before the ask). Typographic treatment, not cards.

**Verify before publishing.** These come from résumés and are presumably lifted from LinkedIn
recommendations. They're real, but Harlan should confirm he's comfortable publishing each name on
a public sales page — a LinkedIn recommendation is a different context from a marketing site.
