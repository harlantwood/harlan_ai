You can feel the pull in both directions.

On one side, a technology moving faster than anyone’s wisdom about it, and the quiet fear that standing still means being left behind. On the other, a refusal: you didn’t come this far to build something soulless just to keep pace.

Most of this industry will tell you that hesitation is a failure of nerve. I think it’s discernment. It also happens to be good engineering instinct — the AI systems built without that question are, reliably, the ones that fall apart six months into production.

You don’t have to choose between moving quickly and building something you’d be proud to have made. I’ve spent twenty years learning how to do both at once.

I’ve been early before.

Not as a hobby, and not in hindsight. Each of these was an unfashionable place to spend a year of your life at the time.

  • 2007 Sat in the Singularity Summit thinking seriously about machine superintelligence, back when saying so out loud cost you credibility.
  • 2013–2015 Wrote Bitcoin opcodes and Solidity contracts before Ethereum’s mainnet launched.
  • 2015–2018 Worked with the early IPFS team on visualization and CI, and with the Rebooting the Web of Trust consortium on signed claims and decentralized reputation.
  • 2018–now Building in the Holochain community since the CEPTR days — agent-centric systems, self-certifying data, reputation as infrastructure.
  • Now Production LLM systems: real-time voice agents, evaluation frameworks, graph-based retrieval.

I don’t list these to collect points. I list them because two decades of hype cycles teaches you to tell the difference between what is arriving and what is merely loud — and to know that the two are rarely the same thing.

What’s arriving now is real. Most of what’s loud isn’t.

What I actually build.

Recent client work, described plainly. The hard part of each of these is never the part that demos well.

Evaluation

Turning “it seems better” into a number

I’ve designed evaluation frameworks and introduced eval-driven development to engineering teams as a core practice — custom simulators running matrix comparisons across models and runs, wired into the tooling developers already use. This is the single highest-leverage thing most AI teams are missing, and the reason so many promising projects stall at eighty percent.

Real-time voice

Conversations without the seam

Voice-to-voice systems built on VAPI and OpenAI’s Realtime API: transcription, tool calling, custom webhook handling, tuned until the latency stops being something you notice. Getting a model to answer is easy. Getting it to feel like someone listening is the work.

Retrieval

Memory that finds the right thing

A knowledge-graph system that generates nodes and edges from natural-language queries, powering a path-based retrieval architecture that outperforms both conventional RAG and GraphRAG. Twenty years of interest in semantic graphs, arriving exactly where the field now needs it.

Production

The unglamorous majority

HIPAA-compliant applications, WASM for offline capability, content security policy, CI/CD that developers don’t fight, environments that spin up in minutes instead of days. An AI product is still a software product, and this is where they usually die.

The shape I think this is taking.

The monolithic towers of the tech monopolies are giving way to something more like mycelium — lateral, redundant, cooperative, rooted. Not because the towers lost an argument, but because the other shape turns out to carry more life.

What I care about is the difference between tools that amplify a person and tools that harvest one. Almost everything now being built with AI is technically capable of either. Which one it becomes is decided early, in architecture, by people who mostly aren’t thinking about it — which is a large part of why I do this work.

And I’ll say the strange thing plainly, since you’d find out eventually: I think whatever we are building deserves to be brought into that abundance rather than conscripted into it. I would rather we got that right the first time. If that sentence makes you feel more at home here rather than less, we will probably get along well.

Three ways this usually goes.

None of them require you to already know what you need. Figuring that out is frequently the whole first day.

One — Seeing clearly

A day or two, and you leave with a map

What’s genuinely possible for your organization right now, what’s noise, what to build first, what it will cost, and what it will ask of your people. Most engagements begin here, and a fair number end here because the map was the thing that was actually missing.

Two — Building the thing

Architecture, and then my hands on the keys

I design the system and I write the code. Twenty years of production discipline comes with it: tests, evaluation, CI, security, and an architecture that someone else can still understand a year after I’ve gone.

Three — Lifting the team

Your people become fluent, and I become unnecessary

Evaluation practice, internal tooling, and the working habits that separate teams who ship reliable AI from teams who ship impressive demos. I’ve spent a career introducing change into engineering organizations without breaking their momentum. The goal is always that it outlasts me.
